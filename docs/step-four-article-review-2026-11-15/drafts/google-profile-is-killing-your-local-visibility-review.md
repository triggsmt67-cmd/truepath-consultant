# Google Business Profile Optimization: A Maintenance Checklist

Google Business Profile Optimization starts with a practical question: can someone looking at your profile tell what you do, where you work, when you're available, and how to reach you? Check those answers against the business you're actually running today.

This guide is for an existing profile that needs maintenance. If you want help reviewing it, see my [Google Business Profile optimization service](https://truepath406.com/services/google-profile).

## Start with the customer request you actually want

Pick one service you genuinely provide. For a plumber, that might be a water-heater replacement rather than every possible plumbing job. Open the public profile on your phone and follow the route a customer would take.

Can they find that service? Does the website link open a page that explains it? Can they reach someone who can discuss the work? Write down the first broken or confusing step.

This is a customer-path check. It isn't a ranking test, and one search from your own phone doesn't represent everyone in town.

## Fix business facts before adding more updates

Compare the profile with your actual operation and website:

- **Name:** use the real business name, without extra city or service keywords.
- **Category:** choose categories that accurately describe the business, using the fewest needed.
- **Location:** represent your real premises or service-area operation accurately.
- **Hours:** correct normal and special hours; don't advertise emergency availability you can't cover.
- **Contact route:** test the phone number and website link yourself.
- **Services:** explain the work you offer and the boundaries that matter to a customer.

Google's [business representation guidelines](https://support.google.com/business/answer/3038177?hl=en) are the reference for identity, categories, and location eligibility. If customers aren't served at your address, review the service-area guidance before displaying it publicly.

Don't add towns as a substitute for a real operating footprint. A service-area list isn't a promise of visibility in each place.

## Separate useful maintenance from ranking promises

Google describes local ranking in terms of relevance, distance, and prominence. Its [local ranking guidance](https://support.google.com/business/answer/7091?hl=en) supports accurate information and genuine reviews; it doesn't establish a weekly inactivity penalty or a guaranteed posting schedule for rankings.

Use photos to answer a customer's question: what does this company do, and what does its work look like? Choose real images you have permission to share. There is no required photo count in this checklist.

Use posts when you have something useful to say, such as a genuine schedule change or seasonal service detail. Google says posts older than six months are archived unless a date range is set. The old seven-day expiry advice is outdated. See [Google's post documentation](https://support.google.com/business/answer/7342169?hl=en).

## Give each maintenance task an owner and a trigger

A practical routine can be small:

- When hours, staffing, phone coverage, or services change, check the profile and website together.
- When you finish work worth showing, save an appropriate photo for a later update.
- When a review needs a useful reply, respond professionally and protect the customer's privacy.
- Once a month, test the public contact route and record what you corrected.

These are suggested operating habits, not Google's ranking requirements. Put one person's name against the routine so it survives a busy week.

For reviews, use a consistent invitation to honest feedback. Don't restrict it to happy customers or request service keywords. The separate [reviews process guide](https://truepath406.com/insights/reviews-feel-awkward-when-theres-no-process) covers the handoff and request wording.

## Measure customer actions without calling them booked jobs

Business Profile Performance can show website clicks and call-button clicks. Google defines the calls metric as clicks on the call button, not confirmed answered calls or bookings. Its performance data can include activity from both organic results and Google Ads. See [Google's metric definitions](https://support.google.com/business/answer/9918094?hl=en).

Keep that report separate from your inquiry and job records. Note the date of each change, then compare similar periods while accounting for seasonality, advertising, and capacity.

For example, a hypothetical HVAC business might correct its holiday hours and discover that the website still advertises a different callback window. Fixing both gives the customer one clear expectation. Any claim about increased bookings would still need job records to support it.
