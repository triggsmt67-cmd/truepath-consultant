# Google Business Profile Optimization: Build a Better Profile

You don't need to be a Google expert to build a strong business profile. You need accurate information, a sensible order of work, and clear descriptions of the jobs you do.

If you can explain your work to a customer over the phone, you already have much of what you need. The next step is putting those answers in the right places.

Here's how I'd work through a profile for a local service business. You can use the same steps to improve one you've had for years.

## 1. Get the business details right

Start with the information someone needs to hire you:

- Your real business name.
- A phone number that reaches the business.
- A working website link.
- Your customer-facing hours, including holiday changes.
- An accurate address or service area.

Use your actual business name. Adding “Best Missoula Plumber” to a name that doesn't include those words can violate Google's rules. If you work from home and only travel to customers, follow Google's service-area guidance and hide the residential address. See [Google's business guidelines](https://support.google.com/business/answer/3038177?hl=en).

If Google is asking you to verify the business, handle that before treating the setup as finished. Also check whether the business already has a profile before creating another one.

## 2. Choose a category that describes the business

The primary category should describe what your business is. Choose the most specific accurate option Google makes available.

Additional categories should describe other real parts of the business. You don't need a category for every job you might accept. Google explains that distinction in its [category guidance](https://support.google.com/business/answer/7249669?hl=en).

This is worth doing carefully. A customer looking for a particular trade should be able to recognize that they're in the right place.

## 3. Write the description from questions customers already ask

Before writing, answer these in plain language:

- What work do you do?
- Who do you do it for?
- Where do you work?
- What should someone know about working with you?

For a fictional plumbing business, that could become:

> We handle drain cleaning, leak repairs, and water heater replacement for homeowners in Missoula. When you contact us, we'll ask what's happening, confirm whether we cover your address, and explain the next step for scheduling a visit.

It tells someone what the company does and what to expect when they call. Compare that with “Your trusted partner for exceptional plumbing solutions,” which could describe almost any plumber and answers very little.

Add details that are true of your business. If you work on older homes, explain the relevant work you handle. If you only take residential jobs, say so. Don't borrow a competitor's promises about availability, experience, or guarantees.

Keep links and promotional offers out of this field. Google's [description rules](https://support.google.com/business/answer/3038177?hl=en) call for useful business information and prohibit links in the description.

## 4. Give each service its own short explanation

Your main description introduces the company. The service entries let you explain individual jobs.

Where your profile has a services section, list the work you actually offer. A useful way to write each entry is to name the job, describe the problem it addresses, and explain what's included.

Two examples for that fictional plumber:

**Drain cleaning:** Clearing slow or blocked household drains, including sinks, tubs, and showers. We assess the blockage and explain the recommended work before starting.

**Water heater replacement:** Removing an existing water heater and installing a replacement. We confirm the equipment and installation scope before providing an estimate.

Use these as examples of structure. Your descriptions should match your own work and process. Google supports service descriptions and, where applicable, prices through its [services editor](https://support.google.com/business/answer/9455399?hl=en).

## 5. Give the website the same clear structure

The website linked from your profile should help someone understand the same business. Keep the services, service area, contact details, and availability consistent.

Start with your main services. Give a service its own page when you have enough useful information to explain the job, show relevant work, and answer the customer's questions. Closely related tasks can share a page when that makes more sense.

**Contact:** 26 Contact details; 27 Request service; 28 Service FAQs.

**Utility pages:** 29 Privacy; 30 Accessibility.

These are possible pages, not a list to fill at any cost. Only publish a case study about work you actually did, and only describe credentials you hold. Add a location page when you serve that place and can offer useful local detail. Swapping town names into the same sales copy adds little for the reader and can stray into the practices Google describes as [doorway abuse](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/essentials/spam-policies).

There is no Google requirement for thirty pages or a separate website page for every service in your profile. This is a planning example, not a ranking formula.

## 6. Show the work, then explain what we're looking at

Choose real photos that help someone understand your business: a completed installation, a service vehicle, or the entrance customers use to find the shop. Get permission where needed and keep private customer information out of view.

For an update, pair a relevant photo with a short explanation. Here's an example you could adapt after a real job:

> Replaced an older water heater in a Missoula home this week. The work included removing the old unit and checking the connections before the replacement went into service.

Only use that wording if it describes the work you did. There is no reason to invent a job story to fill a posting schedule.

Google's [post guidance](https://support.google.com/business/answer/7342169?hl=en) supports updates about the business. Use them when you have something customers need to know, such as a service change, seasonal reminder, or genuine opening in the schedule.

## 7. Make it easy to leave honest feedback

Use a direct review link so customers don't have to search for the right listing. Apply the same invitation rule regardless of whether you expect a glowing review or a mixed one.

A simple request:

> Thank you for choosing us. If you'd like to share your experience, here's our Google review link. Honest feedback is welcome.

Don't offer a reward or ask customers to include service keywords, a staff member's name, or a particular rating. Let them choose what to say. Those limits come from [Google's review policy](https://support.google.com/contributionpolicy/answer/7400114?hl=en).

## 8. Try to hire yourself from your phone

Open the public profile and pick a service you offer. Could someone unfamiliar with your company work out whether you handle it, where you work, and how to contact you?

Tap the website link. Find the service information. Check the phone number and what happens when someone calls during your listed hours.

You may find a simple mismatch: the profile advertises weekend availability, but the website says calls are returned Monday. Correct both so the customer knows what to expect.

Once the basics are in place, keep them current when the business changes. A useful monthly reminder is to check the contact details, hours, links, and reviews. That schedule is a suggestion for staying organized, not a Google ranking requirement.

Google describes local ranking through relevance, distance, and prominence. A clearer profile can help customers understand the business, but it doesn't guarantee a particular Maps position. [Google's local ranking guidance](https://support.google.com/business/answer/7091?hl=en) explains those limits.
