# Reviews Process: Make Honest Feedback Easy After the Job

A useful reviews process has a clear trigger, one owner, and a direct way for customers to leave honest feedback. It should keep working on a busy day without making the front desk pressure someone for a favor.

Keep the practical parts: a visible review link, an optional tap card or QR code, and a well-timed invitation. Let the customer choose whether to review and what to say.

## Build the process around the completed job

Choose an objective trigger, such as a completed service visit or collected vehicle. Apply the same invitation rule to customers with positive, mixed, and negative experiences.

Don't send people through a satisfaction score that directs happy customers to Google and everyone else to a private form. A private support channel can be available to everyone alongside the same public review option.

Google's [review content policy](https://support.google.com/contributionpolicy/answer/7400114?hl=en) prohibits incentives, selective positive-review requests, on-site pressure, and requests for specific review content. That rules out asking customers to mention a service keyword or a technician's name for rankings.

## Use a tap card as an option, not a demand

A tap card uses NFC to open a link on a compatible phone. A QR code provides another route. Both should open the business's actual review page; test them before putting the sign out.

Simple sign wording:

> Want to share your experience? Tap or scan to leave an honest Google review. It's optional, and all feedback is welcome.

Leave the choice with the customer. Don't stand over them, hold up checkout, or require proof that they posted. Offer a normal web link for someone who can't use the card or code.

Google supports [review links and QR codes](https://support.google.com/business/answer/3474122?hl=en). An NFC card is a way to open that link, not a special Google ranking tool.

## Give phone users a clickable link

A QR code in a text can be awkward when the customer is reading on the same phone they'd use to scan it. Use a clickable review link in a message; use the QR code on signage or a printed receipt.

Here's a neutral message for a customer who has agreed to receive this type of text:

> Hi [first name], this is [name] at [company]. Thank you for choosing us. If you'd like, you can leave an honest review here: [review link]. All feedback is welcome. Reply STOP to unsubscribe.

Don't treat an invoice phone number as automatic permission for review-request texts. Check the consent and opt-out requirements of the messaging service you use. For example, [Twilio's messaging policy](https://www.twilio.com/en-us/legal/messaging-policy) requires consent, sender identification, and a working opt-out process; that is provider guidance, not a complete statement of the law.

Choose a reasonable request time after the customer has experienced the work. A next-day request may suit a repair they need to test. That timing is a workflow choice to evaluate, not a universal best-performing hour.

## Assign one owner to check that it works

For a hypothetical auto repair shop, the service manager could own the routine: check the completed-job list, confirm contact permissions, send the standard invitation, and review delivery failures. The front desk can point out the optional sign without carrying a review quota.

Once a week, the owner of the process checks:

- Did the same invitation rule apply to all eligible customers?
- Did the card, code, and link reach the right profile?
- Were opt-outs and delivery failures handled?
- Did public reviews that needed a useful response receive one?

Start with one invitation rather than repeated reminders. Don't chase an individual because you can't find their review; a public display name may not identify the customer, and posting remains their choice.

## Measure the process without scoring the customer

Track invitations sent and successfully delivered. Count new public reviews over the same period, but don't assume each came from that period's invitations. Some people review later or arrive through another route.

Use **delivered invitations ÷ invitations sent × 100** to check delivery. Treat reviews gained per invitation as an approximate period comparison, not a verified individual conversion rate.

Reviews can help prospective customers understand the business. They don't guarantee a position in Maps, and a service-specific sentence isn't a ranking switch.

Audit your last twenty completed jobs. Look for missed invitations, broken links, and inconsistent selection rules. If the wider profile also needs attention, use the [Google Business Profile maintenance checklist](https://truepath406.com/insights/google-profile-is-killing-your-local-visibility).

FAQs (replace the existing FAQ field):

### Can I ask only happy customers for a Google review?

No. Do not use satisfaction screening to route only positive reviewers to Google. Apply an objective invitation rule without discouraging negative feedback.

### Should I ask customers to mention a service or employee?

No. Let customers choose their own words. Google prohibits requests for specific review content, including content identifying a staff member.

### Should a review-request text contain a QR code?

A clickable link is usually easier on the same phone. Use the QR code on a sign or receipt, and send texts only through an appropriate permission and opt-out process.
