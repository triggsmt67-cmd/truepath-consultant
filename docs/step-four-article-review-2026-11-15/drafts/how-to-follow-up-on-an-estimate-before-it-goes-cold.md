# How to Follow Up on an Estimate (Before It Goes Cold)

The estimate isn't the end of the sale. It's the point where the customer decides whether the scope, price, timing, and working relationship make sense.

Useful follow-up helps them make that decision. Confirm they received the quote, agree on the next contact, and make it easy to ask a question or decline. Silence alone doesn't tell you whether the issue is price, timing, another bidder, or something you left unclear.

## Set the next step when you send the estimate

Include a short scope summary, meaningful exclusions, and how to accept or ask questions. State any genuine expiry date or availability limit without inventing urgency.

Then ask when the customer expects to decide and which contact method they prefer. A repair needed tomorrow and a replacement planned for next spring need different follow-up.

For example:

> I've sent the estimate for [work]. It includes [scope] and excludes [important exclusion]. When would you like me to check back, and is email the best way to reach you?

If you use text, make sure the customer has agreed to that contact. Identify the business so a callback or message doesn't arrive without context.

## Use a short sequence with a purpose for each contact

When the customer hasn't chosen a date, the following is a starting workflow to test, not a research-backed optimum:

1. **After sending:** confirm delivery through the agreed channel. An automated delivery receipt doesn't establish that the customer read or understood the quote.
2. **After two business days:** offer help with one likely decision, such as scope, timing, or comparing repair and replacement.
3. **After about a week:** ask whether to keep the estimate open, schedule a later check-in, or close the follow-up.

Use the customer's agreed timing instead whenever you have it. Stop the sequence when they respond, decline, book, or ask you to stop. Don't stack an automatic text on top of a call someone already handled.

## Send something more useful than “just checking in”

**Scope question:**

> Hi [first name], [name] at [company]. Is anything unclear in the [project] estimate? I'm happy to explain what's included before you decide.

**Timing question:**

> You mentioned [timeframe] for the work. Is that still the plan, or would you prefer I check back later?

**Close-the-loop message:**

> Would you like to keep the estimate open, revisit it later, or close the follow-up? Any of those is fine. Let me know what works for you.

Only mention financing, guarantees, or appointment windows when they are real and relevant. Follow-up doesn't give you permission to make a vague estimate sound more certain than it is.

## Make estimates easier to compare

Consider a hypothetical furnace replacement. One quote gives a price and equipment name. Another explains the equipment, installation scope, disposal, exclusions, and how scheduling works.

The second quote gives the homeowner more information for a decision. It may still lose on price or timing. The point is to remove avoidable uncertainty, not claim that communication overrides every other factor.

If a customer asks for a lower number, clarify whether they're comparing the same work. Don't cut the price before you understand the difference, and don't assume every lost estimate was a follow-up failure.

## Measure estimates separately from new inquiries

Create a list of estimates issued during a defined period. Record the date, service, value, agreed next contact, outcome, and reason when known. Keep open estimates visible rather than treating them as losses immediately.

For that group, calculate **accepted estimates ÷ estimates issued × 100** after a suitable decision window. Define how revised or duplicate quotes are counted and use that rule consistently.

Break out urgent and planned work before comparing results. If phone-plus-email follow-up appears to do better than phone alone, check differences in job type and customer preference before crediting the channel.

Research about responding to a new sales inquiry doesn't establish the best schedule for following up on an estimate already issued. Use your own dated records to evaluate this workflow.
