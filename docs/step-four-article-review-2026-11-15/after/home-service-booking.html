The schedule looks thin, so you start thinking about buying more leads. Before changing the budget, I'd pull the last thirty inquiries and find out what happened to them.

How many booked? How many are still deciding? How many wanted work you couldn't fit into the schedule?

Those answers give you something to work on. A booking percentage by itself leaves too much unexplained.

Start with thirty inquiries you can actually follow

Use your call log, form submissions, email, and appointment book. Pick a date range and write down the requests received during it. If the business gets fewer than thirty, use what you have and record the count.

Give each distinct request one row. A homeowner who calls twice and fills out a form about the same water heater is one opportunity, not three.

For this audit, an eligible inquiry is a genuine request for a service you provide in an area you serve. Keep suitable work in the count even when you couldn't offer an appointment. You need to see when capacity is the problem.

Exclude spam, vendor calls, unrelated requests, and repeat contacts about the same job. Keep a separate count of those exclusions.

If you don't know what a missed caller needed, mark eligibility as unknown until you find out. Don't quietly count that call as either a good lead or junk. Show the unknown count beside your rate.

Calculate the home service booking rate the right way around

For this audit, use:

Booked opportunities ÷ eligible inquiries × 100 = booking rate.

Call an opportunity booked when the customer has agreed to a service appointment and you've entered it in the schedule. That is different from a completed job, collected revenue, or an accepted replacement estimate after a diagnostic visit.

Suppose a fictional plumbing business reviews forty contact records. Six are repeat contacts and four are spam or unrelated requests. That leaves thirty eligible inquiries, with twelve booked at the review date.

12 ÷ 30 × 100 = 40% so far.

The rate can change while customers are still deciding. Write down when the inquiries arrived and when you reviewed them. Don't divide this week's bookings by this week's new inquiries if some of those bookings came from last month's requests.

Put the eighteen unbooked inquiries into useful groups

In this example, the business knows the service and location fit from conversations, forms, or voicemail. Here's where those thirty opportunities stand:

  • 12 booked: Confirm the appointment details and keep these opportunities attached to the original inquiry records.
  • 6 still deciding: Record the question or decision they're working through and agree on the next contact.
  • 5 no capacity: The work fit the business, but an acceptable appointment wasn't available. Check scheduling and coverage before purchasing more of the same demand.
  • 4 customer declined: Record the reason the customer actually gave. If they didn't give one, leave the reason unknown rather than writing “price.”
  • 3 no reply yet: Follow up through the agreed contact method and keep the outcome open until you have a reason to close it.

All five groups add up to thirty. Nine inquiries are still open: the six deciding and the three who haven't replied. Calling all eighteen unbooked opportunities “lost jobs” would hide that distinction.

Choose a fix from the reason, not the percentage

If suitable requests sit unanswered, assign someone to return them. Set a response target the team can safely meet and check the actual response times. Don't promise a callback window nobody can cover.

If callers need services you don't offer, look at the page, profile, or ad that brought them in. Clearer service information may save both the customer and your team a wasted call.

If the schedule can't accommodate suitable work, look at staffing, appointment lengths, routing, and the availability you advertise. A full calendar is a different problem from an empty phone log.

If the customer needs an estimate before making a decision, give the quote a clear owner and next step. Agree on the follow-up timing. One unanswered message doesn't tell you they've hired someone else, and repeated empty check-ins don't help them decide.

A useful callback can be simple:

Hi, this is [name] at [company], returning your request about [service]. I wanted to confirm what you need and talk through the next available step. When is a good time to reach you?

Use details and availability that are true. If you contact the customer by text or email, use an appropriate, agreed channel and honor requests to stop.

Check what your dashboard is actually counting

Your notebook and software can disagree because they measure different things.

ServiceTitan's Modular Dashboard defines Call Booking Rate using call leads, with its own duration or lead-marking rules. This audit includes eligible requests across contact methods. Read the definition for the report you're using before comparing the two. ServiceTitan's metric definitions.

Google Business Profile's calls metric counts clicks on the call button. It doesn't establish that someone answered or booked the caller. Google's profile metric definitions.

Google Ads also supports duration-based call conversions. A call meeting your chosen duration threshold is not, by itself, evidence of an appointment. Match the report to your actual booking records. Google's call-reporting explanation.

Keep each measure labeled. You'll learn more from a clear count than from making different reports appear to agree.

Make the next budget decision with the schedule in view

There isn't one booking percentage that tells every service business to increase spending. Review suitable inquiry volume, available appointments, the kinds of jobs you want, and the cost of acquiring them.

Separate urgent repairs from planned projects before comparing results. Give each group an appropriate decision window. Keep the eligibility rules and review timing consistent when you repeat the audit.

Change one clear problem and follow another group of inquiries. A small improvement is worth investigating, but thirty requests aren't proof that a change caused a permanent lift.
