


If you’re paying an agency every month, you shouldn’t have to guess what you’re getting.





You should be able to see what changed, understand why it mattered and know what comes next. A report full of numbers doesn’t help much if it leaves you wondering what any of it means for your business.





The same practical standard applies to your Google Business Profile and website. Customers need to know what you do, where you work and how to reach you. You can improve those basics yourself or hire someone. Either way, the work should make sense.





How can a service business improve its Google visibility?





Start with accurate business information, useful service pages, real job photos and honest customer reviews. Make it easy to contact you, then track whether inquiries become suitable work. If you hire an agency, ask it to show what changed, why it mattered and what comes next. None of this guarantees a ranking.





Start with a realistic goal





Google says local results depend mainly on relevance, distance and prominence. You can’t pay Google for a better local ranking, and maintaining your profile won’t remove the distance between your business and a searcher.





Nobody can honestly guarantee you the number-one spot.





Aim to help more suitable customers find you and take the next step. Are inquiries coming from areas you serve? Do people want work you actually provide? Can they reach you and book an appointment or estimate?





Those questions tell you more than a ranking screenshot by itself.





Make your services easy to understand





Choose the Google Business Profile category that best describes your main business. Google’s guidance is to use a small number of categories that describe the business, rather than one for every service.





Keep your name, phone number, website and hours accurate. Follow Google’s address and service-area rules. Don’t present a virtual office or home address as a storefront customers can visit when it isn’t one.





Use your website to explain the details. An HVAC company should distinguish repairs from replacements. A cleaning company should explain what’s included in a deep clean. A tree company should say whether stump removal is included or separate.





These are useful answers, not just opportunities to repeat keywords.





Show real work and ask for honest feedback





Clear job photos help customers understand what you do. Show a finished installation, a careful equipment setup or an approved before-and-after example. Protect customer information and get permission where needed.





Don’t claim more than a photo proves. Ground-protection mats show a precaution; they don’t guarantee every lawn will remain untouched.





A weekly routine can help you keep up, but Friday uploads aren’t a ranking trick.





Ask customers for honest reviews consistently. Google’s review policy prohibits incentives and selectively asking for positive reviews. Don’t coach people to include certain services, towns or keywords.





Use your profile’s review link and a simple request: “Thanks for choosing us. If you’d like to share your experience, we’d appreciate your honest feedback.”





If you send requests by text, check permission requirements and your provider’s rules first. Respect opt-outs. Reply professionally to reviews without exposing private customer details.





Give each local page a purpose





A useful service-area page helps someone decide whether you can handle their job in their location.





Include real service details, relevant project examples or practical information about estimates and scheduling. Don’t invent offices, projects or local experience.





Swapping town names across nearly identical pages adds little value. Pages built to funnel similar regional searches can also violate Google’s doorway-abuse policy.





Check the contact step, too. Is the phone number easy to find on mobile? Does the form work? Does the customer know what happens after submitting it?





If an agency delivers ten pages, ask what each one helps a customer understand. Page count alone doesn’t establish value.





Expect your agency to explain the work





I don’t think owners should have to learn marketing jargon to understand what they’re paying for.





A monthly retainer can be worthwhile. Technical repairs, useful content and ongoing management take time. But the fee should come with a clear scope and a plain-language account of the work.





I’d expect five things:





What was completed. Show the pages, profile changes and problems fixed. Identify anything still waiting on someone.





Why it mattered. Explain the customer need or business problem the work addressed.





What changed. Show relevant evidence over a stated period. Separate paid and organic activity where possible.





What’s uncertain. Be honest about incomplete tracking, small samples and results that can’t be tied to one change.





What happens next. Name the next priority and anything the owner needs to provide or decide.





Some valuable work won’t produce immediate inquiries. An agency should explain how it will judge that work, too.





“SEO takes time” may be true. It still isn’t a substitute for showing what has been done.





If the explanation is unclear, ask for an example from your own business. You shouldn’t need another meeting just to translate the report.





Follow inquiries through to useful work





Views and clicks can help you understand activity. They don’t prove someone became a customer. Know what the numbers in your Business Profile performance report actually measure.





Keep a simple inquiry record: service requested, location, whether the job was suitable and whether it became an appointment, estimate or booked job. Ask how customers found you, recognizing that their answers won’t always be exact.





Owners have a part in this. Your agency needs feedback about lead quality, available capacity and what happens after the phone rings.





Start by fixing one useful thing. If you’re paying for help, ask your agency to walk through its work and results in plain language. You need enough information to decide what to keep doing, what to change and whether the fee is worthwhile.
